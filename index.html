China Power Spot-Market Analytics

Fourteen provinces publish an electricity price every fifteen minutes. Almost nobody reads them side by side.

  • Consolidated day-ahead and real-time clearing prices across 14+ provincial markets — Guangdong, Shandong, Jiangsu, Zhejiang, Anhui, Shanxi, Ningxia, Xinjiang, Guangxi, Guizhou, Liaoning, Chongqing, Jiangxi and Hebei South — into one comparable time series.
  • Mapped Guangdong’s raw nodal price feed down to city level, so a plant manager could find their own node instead of reading a list of code names.
  • Built a Streamlit tool that ingests a price CSV, auto-detects day-ahead vs. real-time, charts the spread, and exports a standalone HTML report a non-technical colleague can open offline.
  • Compared 3rd vs. 4th regulatory-cycle transmission tariffs to show where industrial retail costs actually move when the schedule changes.
  • Python
  • pandas
  • DuckDB
  • Plotly
  • Streamlit

Client load data is confidential and is not published. What follows is methodology, data model and tool design.

Read the full case study

The problem

China’s electricity market is not one market. It is a couple dozen provincial markets, each with its own trading center, its own file format, its own idea of what a “price” column should be called, and its own website to download it from.

A company shopping for a power contract in Jiangsu has no easy way to ask the obvious question — is this a good price compared to Shandong? — because answering it means twelve downloads, twelve cleanups, and a judgment call about whether you are even comparing the same product.

1. One dataset out of fourteen

I normalized every provincial feed into a single schema:

node_name, running_date, running_time, price

That sounds trivial until you meet the source files. Some provinces publish 15-minute intervals, others hourly. Some ship XLSX with merged header cells, some CSV with a BOM, some a PDF. Several use -999 as a missing-value sentinel that will quietly destroy any average you compute over it.

2. Nodal prices that name a real place

Guangdong publishes prices at the node level — thousands of substation identifiers. Useful to a trader, meaningless to the factory manager who just wants to know what their electricity costs. I built a node-to-city mapping, using pinyin normalization to match inconsistent naming across the feed.

3. An interactive tool, not a one-off chart

The analysis had to outlive me, so I wrote it as a Streamlit application rather than a notebook:

  • Upload one or more price CSVs; the app infers day-ahead vs. real-time from the filename.
  • Upload both and it charts the spread — the number that tells you whether a retailer’s forecasting is any good.
  • Search and select nodes in the sidebar; every chart follows the selection.
  • Export a standalone HTML report — one file, opens offline, no Python needed on the other end.

DuckDB does the aggregation in-process, so filtering millions of interval rows stays responsive without standing up a database server. Results are cached by content hash, so re-uploading the same file is instant.

4. Transmission tariffs: 3rd vs. 4th cycle

Spot price is only part of what a customer pays. I compared the two tariff schedules province by province and voltage level by voltage level, to show where delivered cost actually moves — which is not always in the direction the headline number suggests.

What I took away

  • The cleaning is the analysis. Roughly 70% of the work was reconciling formats and hunting sentinel values. The chart at the end was the easy part.
  • Build the tool, not the answer. My first week produced charts. They were obsolete the moment new data landed. The app was still in use after I left.
  • Domain knowledge is not optional. You cannot spot a wrong number in a price series until you understand why day-ahead and real-time diverge, and how settlement actually works.

On confidentiality. This project used a client’s interval load data for dozens of industrial facilities. None of it — and no customer name — appears here. Methodology and tool design are mine.

Solar Resource & Inter-Annual Risk for Distributed PV

The sun is not the same every year. A twenty-year solar contract is usually priced as if it were.

  • Pulled 26 years of NASA POWER daily irradiance (2000–2026) for 77 industrial rooftop sites — global horizontal, direct normal and diffuse components.
  • Computed a good-year / bad-year coefficient for each site — and found a 15-point spread across the portfolio. Two sites financed on identical assumptions can differ by that much in realized resource in a single year.
  • Audited all 91 project entries against independent geocoding: 42 of them — 46% — were wrong or unverifiable, including two sites logged over 1,100 km from their real location.
  • Benchmarked Yangtze River Delta capitals (Shanghai, Nanjing, Hangzhou, Hefei) on irradiance mix to show why identical panels earn different returns 200 km apart.
  • Python
  • NASA POWER API
  • Excel
  • Geospatial QA
Read the full case study

The question

A rooftop solar developer signs a power purchase agreement that runs for two decades. The financial model behind it rests on one number: how much sunlight the roof will receive. That number is almost always a long-run average.

But no individual year is the average. A cloudy year underproduces, the revenue misses the model, and the person explaining it to a lender discovers that “average” was never a promise. I wanted to put a number on that gap.

1. Twenty-six years of irradiance

I pulled daily surface irradiance from NASA’s POWER API for every project site — 1 January 2000 through mid-2026 — capturing three parameters rather than one:

ParameterWhat it isWhy it matters
ALLSKY_SFC_SW_DWNGlobal horizontalBaseline yield for a flat rooftop array
ALLSKY_SFC_SW_DNIDirect normalDrives tilt and tracking gains
ALLSKY_SFC_SW_DIFFDiffuseSets the floor on overcast days

The raw files arrive with a fourteen-line header block and -999 standing in for missing observations. Averaging over that sentinel without stripping it first produces a confidently wrong answer — exactly the kind of error that survives into a financial model unnoticed.

2. A good-year / bad-year coefficient per site

For 77 sites I computed the ratio of a given year’s irradiance to the long-run mean — a direct multiplier on expected generation:

WindowMinMeanMaxSpread
Calendar 20250.9711.0711.12415.3 pts
Jun 2025 – May 20260.9751.0311.09311.8 pts

The useful finding is the spread, not the mean. If your model treats two sites identically, you have quietly mispriced one of them.

3. The coordinate audit

Before any of that means anything, the latitude and longitude have to be right — irradiance is pulled by coordinate. So I checked all 91 project entries against independent geocoding and each facility’s registered address, logging a deviation in kilometers and a written justification for every one.

42 of 91 entries — 46% — did not survive the check. 36 were outright wrong, 4 could not be verified, 2 were off by a small but non-trivial margin.

SiteRecordedActualError
Glass manufacturerDongguan, GuangdongHuangdao, Shandong1,585 km
Machinery groupWrong provinceChongqing1,160 km
Heavy industryOff-siteAnhui290 km
SanitarywareOff-siteHebei152 km
Auto partsOff-siteHebei114 km

The 1,585 km case is the one I think about. Nothing about the spreadsheet looked broken — it was a valid latitude and a valid longitude, formatted correctly, sitting quietly in the right column. Had it gone unchecked, that site’s twenty-year model would have been built on subtropical southern-China sunlight for a plant that sits well to the north. Every downstream number would have been wrong, and wrong in the optimistic direction.

What I took away

  • Validate the inputs before you model them. The most valuable output was not a coefficient. It was a list of 42 rows that needed fixing.
  • Plausible-looking data is the dangerous kind. A malformed coordinate gets caught. A well-formed wrong one gets used.
  • Show the uncertainty, don’t average it away. A single expected value hides the range a lender actually cares about.

Rules-Based A-Share Trading System & Backtester

A written trading ruleset, compiled into code that enforces it — so the backtest and the live scan can never disagree.

  • Codified a written ruleset into executable form: index-level regime filter, trend and base-structure tests, and a breakout trigger requiring volume confirmation.
  • Position sizing derived from 2% account risk ÷ stop distance, a 30% single-name cap, and a market-breadth gate that shrinks total exposure when few stocks qualify.
  • Built a full-market scanner and a backtester over 2020–present that execute the same rule code — so the historical result reflects what the system would actually have done.
  • Zero third-party dependencies. Concurrent fetching plus a local price cache means a full-market rerun finishes in seconds.
  • Python (standard library only)
  • ThreadPoolExecutor
  • REST APIs
  • Backtesting

Research and programming project. Not investment advice, and no performance claims are made.

Read the full case study

The design goal

Discretionary trading fails in a predictable place: the exit. An entry can be reasoned about calmly in advance; an exit gets negotiated in the moment, against yourself, with money on the line. So I built a constraint engine rather than a prediction engine. The point is to remove the moment where negotiation is possible.

The ruleset, made executable

The system is a written document (now at v0.3) translated clause by clause into testable conditions:

  • Gate 0 — market regime. If the CSI 300 closes below its own 60-day moving average, no new positions open at all. Not smaller ones. None.
  • Gate 1 — trend. Price above its 60-day moving average, and that average itself rising.
  • Gate 2 — structure. A genuine base: closing-price range over the prior 30 sessions within 25%, and price near the 60-day high.
  • Gate 3 — trigger. A close at a new 60-day high on volume at least 1.5× the 20-day average, and no more than 5% above the base’s upper edge — a hard stop on chasing.

Sizing — risk first

shares = (account × 2% risk) ÷ (entry − stop)

constraints:
  single position   ≤ 30% of account
  concurrent holds  ≤ 4
  breadth gate      pass rate < 3%  → max 30% invested
                    pass rate < 10% → max 60% invested

Position size is a function of where the stop sits, not of how much I like the idea — a wide stop automatically buys fewer shares. The breadth gate is the part I am most pleased with: the scanner already knows how many stocks in the whole market pass the filter, and that pass rate is itself a market-health signal. Few qualifiers means a thin market, so the system caps its own exposure without me deciding anything.

Exits

  • Initial stop: the higher of the base’s upper edge × 0.98 and the breakout day’s low.
  • Confirmation: stops trigger on closing price, not intraday spikes.
  • Trailing: once up 15%, the stop switches to a 20-day moving-average trail.
  • Adding: only into strength — up 10% and making a new high — at half size, stop moved to breakeven.

Two programs, one rule engine

This is the design decision I would defend hardest. The scanner and the backtester are not separate implementations that happen to agree — they run the same rule code.

  • Full-market scanner. Pages through every A-share by market cap, drops anything under ¥10bn or flagged ST, applies the gates, and returns three buckets: triggered, watchlist, and broken-below-20-day. It also reports the pass rate feeding the breadth gate.
  • Backtester. Replays 2020 to present on adjusted daily bars, simulating the same gates, sizing, stops and adds, with transaction costs baked into fill prices.

A backtest that uses looser logic than the live scanner is a backtest of a strategy you will never run. Sharing the engine is what keeps the result honest.

Engineering notes

  • No third-party packages. Standard library only — urllib, json, collections, concurrent.futures. Runs on any machine with Python 3 and nothing installed. That constraint was deliberate: it forced me to understand what a library would have hidden.
  • Concurrency and caching. A ThreadPoolExecutor fans out price requests; every series is cached to disk on first fetch. First run takes minutes, every rerun takes seconds.
  • Resilience. Retry with backoff, GBK decoding for the Chinese feeds, and rotation across several API mirrors, because a free data endpoint will rate-limit you halfway through 5,000 symbols.
  • A trade journal. Every position logged with its thesis, its stop, the indicator that would falsify it, the outcome, and one column that matters more than the rest: was this inside the system, yes or no?

What I took away

  • Writing the rule down is most of the work. Turning “cut losses quickly” into a condition a computer can evaluate forces you to state precisely what you meant.
  • Sizing beats selection. Tying position size to stop distance moved outcomes more than any refinement to the entry criteria did.
  • An executable rule is a testable rule. Once the logic is code, you can ask the historical record whether it actually worked — and get an answer you did not choose.
